A lock without a key

A Bitcoin address is rather like a door in a very large city. The blockchain can tell you where the door is, how long it has been shut, and sometimes what is piled up behind it. What it cannot tell you is who has the key—or whether the key is at the bottom of a drawer, in a landfill, or being used as a bookmark.

The private key is the small, inconvenient detail on which the whole business depends. It may be buried in an obsolete backup, imprisoned behind a password its owner once considered unforgettable, or sitting safely in the possession of somebody who has simply decided not to touch their coins. If the key has vanished, the Bitcoin remains visible on the blockchain but may be permanently inaccessible. The transaction history records what happened to the coins; it cannot tell us why an address has gone quiet.

In March 2026, a New York lawsuit tried something rather like claiming every unclaimed suitcase in a railway station. ABC Company, XYZ Company, and Noah Doe v. John Does 1–39,069 asks a court to declare that a pseudonymous plaintiff owns 39,069 dormant addresses—about 3.8 million BTC—without a single private key.

Which raises the awkward question: who owns a Bitcoin nobody can move? The network has a simple test—produce the signature. Between that test and the paperwork sits this audit.

The case points to a larger question: how long must a Bitcoin address remain inactive before we start calling it lost? That question was behind a small project I started in 2018. At the time, I wanted to test whether long-dormant addresses could help identify Bitcoin that might genuinely be lost.

The original idea

In 2018, I built an app to search for Bitcoin that might have been lost. Its working rule was simple: an address with no outgoing transaction for more than six years would be treated as potentially lost. That was a heuristic, not proof of abandonment.

The market had given people plenty of reasons to return to an old wallet. At the start of 2012, one bitcoin was worth roughly $5. It passed $1,000 in 2013, then reached about $20,089 at the December 2017 peak. The 2018 bear market erased more than 80% from that high, but bitcoin still closed the year around $3,743—roughly 800 times its early-2012 price. At the peak, it had been worth more than 4,000 times as much. The assumption was understandable: if someone still had access to a wallet that had appreciated so dramatically, surely at least some owners would have sold.

Eight years later, I returned to that question before deciding whether to revive the project. I wanted to know how many of the old addresses had stayed inactive. If dormancy really was a useful signal of lost access, most of them should still be silent. The result was not quite what that theory predicted.

How we assembled the guest list

The fixed audit sample is a group of 92,076 legacy Bitcoin addresses assembled in 2018. The preserved list examined here used a stricter cutoff than the app’s general six-year screening rule: each address had not sent a single satoshi out for more than seven years. Together, they held 2,530,552.46 BTC at the time—more than one bitcoin in every eight ever mined.

We rechecked all 92,076 addresses. 9,990 had since made an outgoing spend, and 7,816 of those had been emptied completely. The other 82,086 still had no outgoing spend recorded in the audit. That does not establish that they are lost: it only tells us that no valid outgoing transaction has appeared on the blockchain. A spend proves that somebody could produce a valid signature; it does not establish that the original owner still controlled the address.

The ledger of the long-sleeping

About 2.26 million BTC remains in the addresses that never spent. That is 82,086 addresses—89.2% of the list—holding 2,256,802.64 BTC. It is a spectacular amount of money to leave unattended, although “unattended” is not the same thing as “abandoned”. A person can ignore a wallet for fifteen years for reasons ranging from a lost hard drive to a very deliberate investment strategy.

Roughly 245,000 BTC left addresses that proved they could still sign. The outflows came from 9,990 addresses: 9.7% of the snapshot’s value and 10.8% of its addresses. Of these, 7,816 were emptied, 502 were reduced to a tiny residue, and 1,672 made partial exits. The remaining 28,465.27 BTC—1.1% of the original snapshot—still sits in addresses from which somebody has demonstrated an ability to spend.

The year the dead stirred

The year-by-year breakdown tracks the 9,990 addresses that made at least one outgoing transaction, including those that retained a balance after spending.

For seven years, outflows stayed between roughly 11,000 and 32,000 BTC a year. Then 2025 arrived, apparently having something to prove. That year saw 95,845 BTC leave—39% of the audit’s total—with 87,240 BTC coming from just 60 of the 502 near-empty addresses. By the end of 2024, cumulative outflow stood at 140,175 BTC. One day in July 2025 supplied a remarkable chunk of the sequel.

A very long nap

An old address does not necessarily mean every coin inside it is equally ancient. By tracing each spent output back to the transaction that created it, we can measure how long those particular coins remained unspent. Some outputs in this sample had not moved for more than fourteen years.

The 2024 and 2025 examples had waited 13 to 14.5 years. Earlier examples ranged from 5.4 to 11.3 years, with most around seven or eight—the minimum nap required to get onto our original list. In Bitcoin terms, that is a long time.

The July awakening

On 4 July 2025, eight addresses, each containing about 10,000 BTC, began moving in sequence. Between 03:39 and 14:54 UTC they sent out 80,009.33 BTC, leaving behind residues of just 14,000 to 43,000 satoshis. The coins had arrived between 2 April and 4 May 2011. They were 14.2 to 14.3 years old. In this audit, that was not a freak occurrence. It was practically the dress code.

Twenty days later, another large wallet stirred. Address 15MZvKjqeNz4AVz2QrHumQcRJq2JVHjFUz sent 3,962.62 BTC, leaving 16,984 satoshis behind. On 25 July, Galaxy Digital announced that it had sold more than 80,000 BTC—over $9 billion—for a “Satoshi-era investor” as part of estate planning. CoinDesk connected the sale to the July transfers, although Galaxy did not identify the addresses. The first transfers happened on a US holiday, when markets were closed. The timing is striking, but the on-chain data alone cannot identify the person behind the transfers or explain why the coins moved after so long.

Fourteen years of inactivity did not prevent these coins from moving. The transfers prove that somebody could sign. They do not tell us who that person was, or what finally prompted the move.

Other curious resurrections

The totals say how much moved. The individual transactions say how: in batches, on particular days, and sometimes on the very worst day of a bear market. Three of them are worth looking at closely.

26 March 2024 — thirty-four addresses of exactly 50 BTC

Thirty-four wallets holding exactly 50 BTC each emptied in one transaction: 1,700 BTC, plus 1.21 BTC from two smaller addresses. The coins were mined between 7 August and 5 November 2010 and had waited 13.4 to 13.6 years. Fifty bitcoin was the block reward until 2012, and 32,537 addresses in the list held exactly 50 BTC; today 723 have less and 629 are empty. Batches of eight or more such wallets moved together on 24 separate days. That is not 629 independent decisions.

11 November 2022 — seven identical 500 BTC wallets

On the day FTX filed for bankruptcy, seven addresses holding exactly 500 BTC were drained to a residue—3,500 BTC, plus 75 BTC from two fully emptied addresses. Every coin dated to 10 July 2011: 11.3 years dormant. Coins that are genuinely lost do not choose the worst day of a bear market to return.

7 January – 2 April 2019 — the largest single-address exit

1FdjFtrBwf9Jc9fsGN2GtHmG2vs5ZcEuWH held 4,154.25 BTC and left in five transactions across three dates, ending with 1,201.59 BTC that had sat since 19 April 2012. Its last outgoing transaction came in February 2020—a 0.001 BTC dust sweep. Reading only the last spend would miss the twelve-week liquidation entirely.

The ones still sleeping

The bigger story, however, is not the money that moved. It is the money that did not. 82,086 addresses have recorded no outgoing spend and still hold 2,256,802.64 BTC—89.2% of the inventory. They have been silent for at least fifteen years. Some may be lost. Some may be carefully guarded. From the outside, both look remarkably similar.

Most remain almost exactly as they were. 34,824 received only dust and change, adding a mere 43.54 BTC between them over nine years—not the behaviour of a bustling savings account. 94 addresses still hold at least 1,000 BTC, together accounting for 344,864 BTC, or 15.1% of the balance still represented in the list. There are fortunes here large enough to make a pirate reconsider the sea.

The largest is 1FeexV6…: 79,957 BTC, untouched since March 2011. It is also John Doe #1 in the New York case, identified by Galaxy Research as the address holding coins stolen from Mt. Gox. This is not simply a dormant balance: it is stolen, contested property that investigators and creditors have tracked for fifteen years.

So can we call the remaining 82,086 addresses lost? Their long inactivity makes that tempting. Some of the outputs associated with this group have remained unspent for around fourteen years or more. But even that is not a final answer. A separate spot-check of activity during the seven days ending 22 November 2026 found that addresses still described as “lost” were continuing to move. Long dormancy is evidence of inactivity, not proof that nobody has the key.

What should the threshold be: six years, ten, fourteen, or longer? There may be no universal number. “Lost” is not a status the blockchain can report. It is an inference about the person who may—or may not—still control the key.

Can silence be legally interpreted?

The New York suit rests on a lost-property theory. Noah Doe says an algorithm flagged addresses that had been quiet for five years or more, that he reported them to the police, and that tiny payments carrying an “abandonment notice” were sent to each one in mid-2025. Nobody answered within 90 days, so he claims title. The complaint now names 39,069 addresses; the case is still pending, and nothing below is a legal finding. Our audit suggests four reasons to be sceptical.

Dormancy is not abandonment. Roughly one address in nine on our list eventually spent something. The 4 July 2025 transfers are the grandest example: fourteen years of silence, then a professional sale through a regulated desk.

A notice nobody can see is hardly a notice. Most wallets don’t display messages attached to a transaction, and many filter out a 546-satoshi payment as spam. The plaintiffs have already dropped 44 defendants whose addresses moved, so some owners were clearly reachable. They just didn’t reply the way the notice expected.

The guest list is awkward. It includes the Mt. Gox hacker address, a Counterparty burn address that was never owned by anyone, and roughly 21,923 addresses associated with Satoshi. Lost-property law struggles to make one neat parcel out of stolen coins, historic coins, and coins with no owner at all.

A judgment is not a key. Even a complete victory would produce a declaration on paper, not the private key needed to move a coin.

The numbers do not sit comfortably together. The legal lever is a provision for property worth under $10, yet 39,046 of the 39,069 addresses are worth far more than that today. The median holds about 50 BTC—roughly $3.86 million. A court can settle an argument about paperwork; it cannot make a single satoshi budge.

We are not lawyers; this is research, not legal advice.

The hunt for the key

And here, usually, the story puts on its coat and goes home. The court may decide who has a claim; the blockchain still requires a valid signature. A title deed cannot sign a transaction. Only the private key can do that. Which leaves us with a question more interesting than the lawsuit: what if the key has not been destroyed, but merely misplaced in the enormous digital equivalent of the sofa cushions?

If every private key were generated perfectly at random, the answer would be no. Not “unlikely”, not “you might need a better phone”, but no in the practical sense that you cannot walk to the Moon by taking very small steps. A private key comes from roughly 2256 possibilities; for a legacy address, the matching target is a 160-bit hash. In a simplified model of independent guesses, the chance of matching one specified address is about 1 in 2160—roughly 1 in 1.46 × 1048. Even at a billion guesses a second, the expected wait is about 4.6 × 1031 years. Your phone would become an heirloom long before it found anything.

But history is full of software that was less random than it believed, passwords that were more memorable than secure, dice that did not have enough sides, and shortcuts that seemed clever right up until they became evidence. Some keys may have been generated by flawed random-number generators, derived from weak phrases, reused, copied, or truncated. In those cases, the key may lie in a much smaller region than the full mathematical universe. A search of that region is not the same thing as breaking Bitcoin. It is looking for the places where human beings, being human beings, took a shortcut.

There is an important distinction here. A dormant address is not necessarily lost, and a lost key is not necessarily recoverable. The fact that an address has remained untouched for years does not make its private key easier to guess. Searching a limited set of historically plausible weak-key patterns is fundamentally different from trying random keys until one works.

The odds of finding a key through searching remain extraordinarily low unless the relevant key lies in the particular region being examined. Most searches will find nothing. No method can promise a windfall. Still, the audit gives us a reason to keep asking questions: 9,990 addresses once classified as dormant eventually demonstrated that somebody could spend from them. That does not mean their keys were waiting nearby. It does mean that “quiet” and “gone” are not synonyms.

And if you find a key, does that make the coins yours? Not necessarily. Control of a key and legal ownership are different questions, as the New York case rather dramatically demonstrates. If a match ever appeared, establishing lawful ownership would come before anything else.

How long must a Bitcoin address remain dormant before we can call it lost—six years, ten, fourteen, or perhaps forever? This audit cannot give us a magic number. It can show why any cutoff should be treated as a working definition rather than a verdict. Addresses that look abandoned can move again; addresses that remain silent may still have someone watching over them. For now, the most accurate word is not “lost”, but “dormant”.
