The mystery of lost keys

Somewhere on Bitcoin's public ledger sit fortunes that have not been spent for years. Everyone can see the balances. The keys that could unlock them are another story.

A key might be buried in an old backup, locked behind a forgotten password, or held by someone perfectly content to wait. If it is gone for good, the bitcoin stays visible but unreachable. The blockchain leaves no message telling us which story is true.

Bitcoin Key Hunter began with the idea of searching for lost bitcoins. Here, presumed lost refers to addresses with no outgoing spending over the period under study. Coins that remain unspent for many years may indeed be lost, but that is a hypothesis, not a statistically established conclusion. A patient holder can look exactly like someone who has lost a key. A spend resolves the question in one direction: someone could still authorize a transaction.

Where the list came from

This was no random sample. The Bitcoin Key Hunter app ships with a list built in 2018 using one stark filter: legacy 1… addresses that had not sent a single satoshi out for more than seven years.

Seven years was already a long silence in 2018, reaching back to roughly 2011 or earlier—before the first halving and into Bitcoin's earliest years. The list contained 92,083 addresses with recorded balances totaling about 2.53 million BTC, more than one bitcoin in eight ever mined. By November 2026, addresses with no outgoing spend had been inactive for at least fifteen years, some for nearly Bitcoin's entire life.

The audit asked two questions: Is the money still there? And if not, when did it leave? All 92,083 balances were checked. Transaction histories showed outgoing activity for 9,990 addresses; 7,816 were fully emptied, while the others retained some balance. The remaining addresses had no outgoing spend recorded.

The ledger

About 2.26 million BTC remains in the no-outgoing-spend group. It is held across 82,093 addresses—89.2% of the original inventory. Long inactivity may point to lost keys, but it cannot rule out owners who have chosen not to move their coins.

Roughly 245 thousand BTC left addresses that demonstrated access to their keys. The outflows came from 9,990 addresses—9.7% of the snapshot's value and 10.8% of its addresses. Of those, 7,816 were fully emptied, 502 retained only a small residue, and 1,672 made partial exits. The remaining 28,465.27 BTC, or 1.1% of the snapshot, stayed in partially spent addresses.

When the lost came back

The year-by-year view follows all 9,990 addresses with outgoing activity, including those that retained a residue or made only partial exits.

For seven years, annual outflow generally ranged from about 11,000 to 32,000 BTC. Then came 2025: outflow rose to 95,845 BTC, or 39% of the audit's total, with 87,240 BTC coming from just 60 of the 502 near-empty addresses. By 2024, cumulative outflow had reached 140,175 BTC; one July 2025 day accounts for much of the subsequent jump.

The July 2025 transfers

On 4 July 2025, eight addresses that had each held around 10,000 BTC showed a combined balance decrease of approximately 80,009.33 BTC, leaving small positive balances. Two much smaller addresses also moved coins that day, bringing the day’s total to about 80,010 BTC across ten addresses. Their highlighted outputs had been funded between April and May 2011 and were roughly 14.2 to 14.3 years old. Twenty days later, on 24 July, another large address moved: 15MZvKjqeNz4AVz2QrHumQcRJq2JVHjFUz, with 3,962.62 BTC leaving and just 16,984 satoshis behind. Its highlighted output dated to 15 January 2011; it was one of eighteen near-empty addresses that once held at least 1,000 BTC, and the second-largest after the 4 July transfers.

On 25 July, Galaxy Digital announced a sale of more than 80,000 BTC for a “Satoshi-era investor” as part of estate planning. CoinDesk later linked the sale to the July transfers, but Galaxy did not identify the eight addresses. The public disclosure and on-chain movements are therefore connected by reporting, not by an address attribution. The motive for the 24 July transfer also remains undisclosed.

Timing is context, not proof of motive. A transaction shows that someone could authorize a spend. It does not reveal who controlled the key, whether the coins were sold, or why they were moved.

26 March 2024 — thirty-four addresses of exactly 50 BTC

Thirty-four addresses holding exactly 50 BTC each emptied in one transaction, moving 1,700 BTC. Two smaller addresses added 1.21 BTC, bringing the day to 1,701.21 BTC from 36 addresses. The 50 BTC outputs were created between 7 August and 5 November 2010 and had waited 13.4 to 13.6 years. Fifty bitcoin was the block reward until the first halving in 2012; across the full inventory, 32,537 addresses held exactly 50 BTC. At the later check, 723 had a smaller balance and 629 were empty.

Between November 2018 and September 2026, batches of eight or more such wallets moved together on 24 separate days, including 18 on 24 November 2019, 30 on 1 March 2024, 34 on 26 March 2024, 28 on 22 May 2024, and 30 on 10 January 2026. The recurring batches may reflect a smaller number of operators using mining-era keys, perhaps through an exchange or custodian process; no operator has been identified.

24 January 2020 — three wallets, two transactions

1GW9vm3Q… (641 BTC), 12th9wa1… (625), and 1KdhAg4Z… (623) left on a Friday afternoon. The 625 BTC slice went first at 15:48 UTC; the other two followed at 16:22, just 34 minutes later. All three had been funded on 27 May 2012, making the selected coins 7.7 years old. The oddly sized slices may represent remnants of earlier positions moved on one operator's schedule; two transactions do not necessarily imply two controllers.

11 November 2022 — seven identical 500 BTC wallets

On the day FTX filed for bankruptcy, seven addresses holding exactly 500 BTC each were drained to a residue: 3,500 BTC, plus 75 BTC from two fully emptied addresses. Their selected outputs were all created on 10 July 2011, making them 11.3 years old. The coincidence makes the date notable, but the on-chain record shows what moved and when—not who moved it or why.

7 January–2 April 2019 — the largest single-address exit

1FdjFtrBwf9Jc9fsGN2GtHmG2vs5ZcEuWH held 4,154.25 BTC, the largest balance among the addresses eventually emptied. It left in five transactions across three dates: 2,001.71 BTC on 7 January 2019, 950.95 BTC on 13 January, and 1,201.59 BTC on 2 April. That largest output was created on 19 April 2012 and had waited 7.0 years. The address's final outgoing transaction was not until 3 February 2020, when a 0.001 BTC dust sweep occurred. Looking only at the last-spend date obscures the main exit; the five larger spends instead show a staged liquidation over twelve weeks.

Three kinds of coincidence

Market stress. September 2018's crypto winter; November 2022's FTX filing; September 2020's tech-stock selloff, when 1,188 BTC left on the 4th. These dates are consistent with moments when a holder with a working key may have had a reason to move. The uncomfortable point remains: genuinely lost coins cannot choose the worst day of a bear market to return.

Institutional plumbing. The 34 × 50 BTC batch on 26 March 2024 could reflect a custodian or exchange running a batch job. ETF flows turned positive the same day. The timing may point to a shared institutional calendar, but it does not show that one event caused the other.

Nothing at all. The largest movement—80,010 BTC across ten addresses, roughly a third of all outflow—fell on the US holiday of 4 July 2025; eight wallets accounted for 80,009 BTC of it. The size was striking, but there was no clear headline catalyst. Possible explanations include a custodian changing systems or someone demonstrating access to keys presumed gone. Neither explanation is established, and the later Galaxy disclosure does not identify the eight addresses.

The coins that stayed

The larger number belongs to the coins that stayed: 82,093 addresses had no outgoing spend recorded, holding about 2.26 million BTC—89.2% of the inventory. They had been inactive for at least fifteen years.

Among the funded addresses in this group, 47,262 had exactly the same balances, totaling 83,623.67 BTC. Another 34,824 increased by a combined 43.5 BTC through incoming change and dust.

The audit also identified 94 addresses still holding at least 1,000 BTC each, for 344,864 BTC in total—15.1% of the surviving set. Four held more than 10,000 BTC; their balances appear below. Together, those four account for 149,879 BTC, with only small increases consistent with incoming dust and payments rather than withdrawals.

Can lost bitcoins be found?

The ledger is public, and anyone can explore old addresses and follow their transactions. In principle, guessing a key that unlocks an address could give access to its coins. But a balance is visible to everyone while the key's accessibility may remain unknown until an authorized transaction occurs; the odds make random guessing almost unimaginably difficult.

Bitcoin private keys are chosen from roughly 2256 possibilities. For a typical legacy pay-to-public-key-hash (P2PKH) address, a private key is used to derive a public key, then a 160-bit hash that identifies the address. Under a simplified model where each new, valid private-key guess produces an independent, uniformly distributed hash, the chance that one guess matches one specified address is about 1 in 2160.

That is roughly 1 in 1.46 × 1048 per guess. As a purely theoretical scale comparison, at a constant rate of one billion independent guesses per second, the expected time to match one specified address would be about 4.6 × 1031 years.

So, can lost bitcoins be found? Technically, yes. Practically, you would need a level of luck that makes winning the lottery look like a routine daily event.
