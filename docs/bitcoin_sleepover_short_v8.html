The Great Bitcoin Sleepover

Thousands of supposedly forgotten Bitcoin wallets have been quietly doing what forgotten things do best: waiting. Some of them have started to wake up. An audit of 92,076 old addresses looks for the people behind them, and for what is revealed and what stays hidden when one wakes up.

The thread through this piece: old coins can wake up, and when they do, the chain shows that someone could sign—but never who.

A lock without a key

A Bitcoin address is rather like a door in a very large city. The blockchain can tell you where the door is, how long it has been shut, and sometimes what is piled up behind it. What it cannot tell you is who has the key—or whether the key is at the bottom of a drawer, in a landfill, or being used as a bookmark.

In March 2026 a New York lawsuit asked a court to hand 39,069 dormant addresses, about 3.8 million BTC, to a pseudonymous plaintiff, without a single private key. Which raises an awkward question: who owns a Bitcoin nobody can move?

The audit

In 2018 I built an app that treated any address with no outgoing transaction for six-plus years as probably lost. The market had given owners plenty of reasons to come back: bitcoin went from about $5 in early 2012 to about $20,000 at the December 2017 peak. If dormancy really meant “lost”, nearly every address on the list should still be silent.

Eight years later I rechecked the preserved list: 92,076 legacy addresses, each silent for more than six years back then, together holding 2,530,552 BTC. That is more than one bitcoin in every eight ever mined.

The result was not quite what the theory predicted.

What woke up

82,086 addresses (89.2%) have still never spent and hold 2,256,802.64 BTC. Whether that is abandonment or a very deliberate investment strategy, the blockchain cannot say.

9,990 addresses (10.8%) did spend. But the big balances mostly stayed put: about 245,000 BTC left, only 9.7% of the list’s value. A spend proves that somebody could produce a valid signature. It does not prove the original owner was the one signing.

Outflows were fairly steady for seven years, at roughly 11,000–32,000 BTC a year. Then came 2025: 95,845 BTC left, and 87,240 of it came from just 60 addresses. As the next section shows, the July transfers alone moved 80,009 BTC.

The big wake-ups

These coins were old. The 2024 and 2025 transfers had waited 13 to 14.5 years; earlier ones mostly sat for seven or eight.

On 4 July 2025, eight addresses holding about 10,000 BTC each sent out 80,009 BTC over eleven hours, after 14.2 years of silence. On 25 July, Galaxy Digital announced it had sold more than 80,000 BTC (over $9 billion) for a “Satoshi-era investor”, and CoinDesk linked the two. Galaxy never named the addresses, so the chain alone cannot say who was behind the transfers.

On 26 March 2024, thirty-four wallets holding exactly 50 BTC, the 2010 block reward, were emptied in a single transaction after about 13.5 years. Eight or more such wallets moved together on 24 separate days. That is not hundreds of independent decisions.

On 11 November 2022, seven identical 500 BTC wallets were drained on the day FTX filed for bankruptcy, after 11.3 years. The timing looks suspicious, but that is all it is. An heir, a custodian or someone who found a backup could move coins on any day.

November: the peel chain

After the main audit closed on 11 November, a one-week review (12–19 November) caught 19 more old addresses spending: 241.4 BTC in total, after 13.9 to 16.2 years of silence. All were funded between 2010 and 2012.

Then the coins kept moving. They hopped from address to address in a pattern called a peel chain.

What is a peel chain?

Bitcoin coins are spent whole, like banknotes. If you pay for a coffee with a €500 note, you hand over the entire note and get change back. In Bitcoin the change goes to a brand-new address that your wallet creates for you.

A peel chain is that, repeated again and again. Take a big pile. Send a small slice somewhere (the “peel”) and send the rest to a fresh address. At the next hop, do the same. The pile shrinks and the trail gets longer.

How it could hide who the owner is

Background, not a finding. This is how peel chains are generally used. The review did not find evidence that anyone was hiding.

A peel chain does not make coins invisible, because the blockchain is public. But it can make the owner hard to find. Here are the reasons someone might use one:

  1. Every hop is a new address, so the last one looks unrelated to the first unless you walk the whole chain. That is tedious for a human.
  2. Nothing on-chain says which output of a transaction is the payment and which is the change. Analysts guess, usually that the biggest output is the change, which is how I traced these paths. A chain built to look odd can send that guess down the wrong branch.
  3. The main trail often ends up in a big exchange or broker wallet, where it mixes with thousands of other customers’ coins. In the reviewed paths, 91 of 134 steps came after such a jump, when the traced amount suddenly swelled to thousands of BTC that were obviously not the original holder’s. Past that point the trail is mud.

Cashing out slice by slice also avoids one giant, attention-grabbing sale.

The same pattern also appears innocently: wallets create change outputs all the time, and services batch payments (125 of the 134 transactions had more than one output). And a chain that ends at a regulated exchange can still lead investigators to a name.

Why dormant coins are ideal for this

If you wanted to move coins without being identified, the best coins to hold are the ones nobody has looked at in fifteen years.

Start with the missing paper trail. Coins from 2010–2012 were often mined directly or bought before ID-checked exchanges existed, so there is no account, email or verification record tying the address to a person. The public clustering data I checked found no real-world identity for any source or final address.

There are no habits to match either. Active wallets leave fingerprints: the same exchange, similar amounts, similar timing. An address silent for 14 years has no history to compare against, so its first move is the first clue anyone gets.

And the key-holder could be anyone: the original miner, a buyer, an heir, a custodian, someone who found an old backup, or a thief. Having the key tells you someone can spend the coins. It tells you nothing about who they are. After this long, “who are you?” is a question the chain cannot answer.

Waking up is loud. Trackers watch these addresses, and the move is public within minutes. What a peel chain could protect is the identity of whoever moved the coins, and where they ended up.

What we can’t tell

What the chain does not say:
  • Who signed. The signature shows that someone had the key, not who they are.
  • Why the coins moved. A holder tidying old funds, a custodian migrating wallets, an heir, a thief, or someone hiding. The data fits all of them.
  • Whether the coins are still the original owner’s. Holding a key and owning the coins are different questions.

The ones still sleeping

94 addresses still hold 1,000 BTC or more, 344,864 BTC between them. The largest, 1FeexV6…, holds 79,957 BTC untouched since March 2011. It is also “John Doe #1” in the New York case: the address Galaxy Research identifies as holding coins stolen from Mt. Gox. Long dormancy is evidence of inactivity, not proof that nobody has the key.

The court case

The suit rests on a lost-property theory. In May 2026, with court permission, the plaintiffs sent 546-satoshi payments with an attached legal notice to the addresses, though many wallets do not even display such messages. The case is pending, and nothing here is a legal finding.

Dormancy is not abandonment: about one address in nine on our list eventually spent something. The list is also awkward. It includes the Mt. Gox hacker address, a Counterparty burn address nobody ever owned, and about 21,923 addresses associated with Satoshi. And a judgment is not a key. Even a total victory yields a paper declaration, not the signature needed to move a coin.

The numbers do not sit comfortably together. The legal lever is a provision for property worth under $10, yet 39,046 of the 39,069 addresses are worth far more. The median holds about 50 BTC, roughly $3.86 million.

We are not lawyers; this is research, not legal advice.

The hunt for the key

Given the disclosure above, read this section skeptically: nothing here suggests a key will turn up.

The court may decide who has a claim, but the blockchain still wants a signature. So what if the key was not destroyed, just misplaced?

If every key were perfectly random, the answer would be no. Matching one specific legacy address is about a 1 in 2160 shot, roughly 1 in 1048. At a billion guesses a second, that is on the order of 1031 years.

The only hope is that some keys are not random: flawed generators, weak phrases, reuse. Those live in a much smaller region, and searching it is not breaking Bitcoin. But most searches will find nothing, and a dormant address is no easier to guess than any other.

And if you find a key, are the coins yours? Not necessarily. Holding a key and legally owning the coins are different questions, as the New York case demonstrates.

The question still remains: how many of these addresses are truly lost? And how long must a Bitcoin address stay dormant before it can finally be pronounced lost—forever?
